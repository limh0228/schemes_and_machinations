Growing up in the myth of an all-powerful and all-loving God like the Christian one sets you up for legendary heartbreak. The first time I broke up with my God, I was in high school. I felt immense jealousy toward the people who still believed that the universe was created by a loving being, and not simply a being who loves, but who loves me, specifically. That delusion is the most beautiful thing we have ever made, and I still reach for it all the time. 

<br>

Personalization of love (like all my other psychological deficiencies) appears to be a learned rationalization from childhood when those who controlled my physical universe by all practical means did specifically love me. My mother and my father were my creators. They fabricated my flesh. They decided what I ate and saw, when I slept and woke, and in no uncertain terms, dictated at every moment, whether I would live or die. They were by very definition gods who specifically love me (if only gods of a limited domain). 

<br>

One alternative theory is self-apotheosis: God exists, and She is Me. Believing I am Jesus, a prophet, or a chosen divine figure is one of the best-documented delusions in schizophrenia. Not because religion causes the illness, but because religious figures are ready-made symbols of ultimate moral authority, historical significance, and a private line to the truth. When the inside of my head feels revelatory and universe-shaping, self-appointed apotheosis is an efficient plot container to respond to the terrifying question: why does everything feel so important? The answer fits the size of the feeling: because I am central to the story.

<br>

The very definition of “self” is a similar religious delusion. “I” can kill things. “I” can love them. “I” appear to exist, and “I” appear to be devastatingly important. Aside from self as individual, there are varying perceptions of self including family (clearly we imagine families as being members of our selves, we even share the identifiers of name), twosomes (couples being one of the most popular structures of “selfhood”) and larger collective selves like neighborhoods, races and nations. Usually, it is convenient to conform to the naturally emergent conception of self whose most obvious shape is the singular body. My body, after all, is the one through which all of life appears to be sensed.  This gets us well enough through life, and importantly, it at least appears to make sense. 

<br>

In Islam, the doctrine of tawhid (Oneness of God) strictly disallows me from associating with the divinity of God. It is unfavorable to claim that I am Allah, or to engage in the self-aggrandizing delusion that I am not only solely responsible for the collective fate of humanity, but actually capable of changing it. Some Sufis get around this mess by the philosophy of Wahadat al-wujud according to which the monotheistic God is identical with existence and this one existence is that through which all existing things exist. This means, not that I am God, but that I am of God, if and only if everything else also is of God, to the same exact extent. We are all One in God, which explains how we might love each other (or in other words, love ourselves, as a religious act of collective transcendence).

<br>

The popular Christian theory argues that God’s will is simply carried out through the materials of earthly things such as plants, the weather, and us. We are not God, but we are the actors of Her Will. It’s another bargaining idea which attempts to explain the essential Christian paradox: the apparently limited nature of God’s supposedly unlimited power. 

<br>

But all philosophies of self, not just the monotheistic religious ones, have to, in some way, explain the incredible grief of human helplessness in the face of human suffering. Contending are the two apparent realities that I am a member of the most powerful animal on Earth, and at the same time that I am daily powerless against enormous concerts of human-invented suffering. This basic contradiction makes me feel like my skin has been made suddenly inside out, and like I am raw to every slightest wind and light.

<br>

Experts often cite personalization (and personalized theories of persecution) as one of the primary conditions necessary for genocide:

1)	Obviously, massive power must aggregate. 

2)	Human beings must feel fear. 

3)	They must fear outsiders. 

4)	But finally, and perhaps most critically, they must personalize that fear. They must imagine that their “in-group” is existentially threatened by the “out-group” and that the “in-group” specifically deserves to exist. 

<br>

It seems that once the self emerges as a framework of perception, threats to the self naturally justify in turn, threats to the other – things that are not me. Is it actually possible to transcend personalization? Or do we just elect for more and more “useful” i.e. “collective” ideas of selfhood, until all living things are “me” and therefore deserving of dignity?

<br>

I thought about all these questions again, midday in May, high as balls on the acid tabs. I sat on top of a boulder off the West Manhattan Riverside Parkway with three friends, overlooking 30 or 40 children between five and ten years of age running around in an enclosed playground about the size of a basketball court. Sachi observed that there are certainly bullies down there, and that we were witnessing a complex and self-contained ecosystem of hardship, growth, and of course, play. The playground terrain was generously diverse: fountains, plastic boulders, red slides, blue swings, snacks, gravel, benches, sand, the works. The nannies and moms sat around the peripheries sweating through their shirts.

<br>

I said to Daniel, if I was God, all the world would be a playground. The ground would only be hard enough to learn the lesson of the fall, but soft enough to go on. All the bullies would only be slightly taller than us. Difficulties would be didactic and aptly measured according to our abilities, such that we could surmount them and come out pleasantly emboldened, overcome. Mothers and nannies would always be within a second’s reach. There would always be someone nearby to save us. If I was God, the world would be safer and more fun, which is as good a proof as any that I don’t appear to be Her. 

<br>

But I do love people. I am no longer Christian, but still have a relatively devotional and human-centric stance. I love the other as I did once love God, and I feel that if there is anything called God, that love must be some expression of it. It also seems that all human sicknesses exist inside of me. Inside every horrible collective human impulse (slavery, empire, war, genocide), I see myself. I see that all humanity’s evil is identified in the composition of my spirit. I hate myself every day. I hate others every day. I am trying whatever I can to lend us all the dignity and love I feel we deserve. But I fail to do this all the time, at every single level. I still don’t understand. It’s possible that I never will. This essay has no coherent thesis. I’m trying to accept this.
