<br>

In February, the Guardian reported from an undisclosed leak source that ICE had “more than tripled the amount of data it stored in Microsoft’s Azure cloud platform in the six months leading up to January 2026, a period in which the agency’s budget swelled and its workforce rapidly expanded, according to the files.” By this time, Microsoft had famously been providing cloud support for the Israeli Defense Force for several years through the genocide, and had already experienced some public backlash for this financial support of the Apartheid in Gaza.

<br>

More than a focused hatred for these bad actors called “Microsoft” or “DHS”, my love and respect for my colleagues and myself beleaguered me most. The abject feeling of powerlessness to witness the ways we made sense of ourselves and our work crept into everything. It is actually a painful question: How do normal people end up in jobs building infrastructure for mass-surveillance technologies for a hostile state? And not just normal people, but many tech workers are themselves immigrants and migrants.

<br>

Omniscient and malignant surveillance culture is perhaps taken for granted by those outside the tech sector who have become accustomed to the reality of Ring cameras and police surveillance towers and even good old-fashioned police foot patrols, but the ability to take a photo of someone’s face -- as ICE officers do using the Mobile Fortify app -- to identify their name and immigration status, which is then linked with their address of residence, phone records, family relationships, and biometric data all in the present moment of the interaction is actually very exceptional. This capability requires an incredible technological infrastructure of immense scale and complexity, which only humankind could be capable of building. No other animal could achieve this.

<br>

There are only a few companies in the world which can provide datacenters capable of hosting that much data and relating or processing it at such scale and speed. Datacenter resources at this scale are sold in a very narrow market. If a few companies neglected to take these lucrative hosting contracts with ICE and DHS, other private companies would undoubtedly take the bids, but they would cost themselves years and hundreds of millions of dollars to provide access to comparable resources and services on par with the top three cloud providers: Microsoft, Amazon and Google. A denial of service by just three companies would not stop the mass deportations, but it would definitively delay the administration’s efforts by years and/or hundreds of millions of dollars. Perhaps in that time, human rights lawyers could have recouped more resources to support displaced migrants. Perhaps in that time, international courts could have grown a set of balls which they might use to bring the administration to justice for any number of its recent global atrocities. We know this delay would equal lives saved. We all know this.

<br>

The refusal of cooperation by just these three private companies could set back the rise of American fascism and degradation of global civil liberties to a strangely significant degree. This is deeply concerning for two reasons: firstly that an actor, and a centralized, non-human actor, a corporation, of all things, is capable of threatening our practiced concept of civil liberties is a sign of wicked sickness. Microsoft has no investment in human well-being and only seeks to live and grow, like a cancer, at all costs even to its own host. It should never have had access to Weil’s force in the first place. The second reason for concern, is that all three actors, given the choice, have of course, done nothing about it.

<br>

On February 27th, I was still working at Microsoft. I turned 25 and the United States and Israel began airstrikes in Iran. Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and several members of his family were killed. In Tehran, missile strikes were reported to have occurred on University Street, in the Jomhouri area, and in the northern Seyed Khandan area. On February 28th, The Shajareh Tayyebeh girls' elementary school in Minab, Iran, was struck by a missile. Iranian media reported 180 killed. 

<br>

On March 19th, The United Nations stated in a humanitarian situation report that families across the Occupied Palestinian Territory observed the month of Ramadan. It continues:

<br>

“The UN Human Rights Office had raised alarm over a pattern of unlawful killings following incidents on 15 March in which two Palestinian families were reportedly killed by Israeli fire in Gaza and the West Bank. The office warned that these incidents, alongside escalating settler attacks and intimidation against Palestinian communities, raise serious concerns about impunity and protection risks for civilians. In the Gaza Strip, most people remain displaced, often living in dire conditions with limited protection and inadequate access to diverse and sufficient supplies, while continuing to face daily shooting and strikes hitting residential areas.” 

<br>

“In the West Bank, displacement linked to settler attacks and access restrictions up to March, 2026 has already reached about 95% of the total numbers recorded in the whole of 2025.” 

<br>

The report continued to note that since the announcement of the ceasefire and the brokerage of the Peace Deal by the Peace Board (a multinational initiative chaired by Trump) in October 2025, 673 Palestinians had been killed.

<br>

A hopeful far-right authoritarian regime like ours benefits from senseless global conflict in two ways: 

1) Fear of outsiders escalates during wartime, so that civilians can be goaded and manipulated into engaging with xenophobia, and 

2) the scale of contemporary warfare including the tertiary civilian violence is incomprehensible. 

<br>

Coexistence with the fact of war and genocide is itself traumatic, and causes reality’s seeming rules of order to fracture into irrationality. Why should children die in a war they did not create? Of course, there’s no such reason for this, and seeing that reality does not itself abide by humane rules, the civilian populace is negged to angry, confused psychosis which taken to its extent, manifests as numbness. This high level of daily senselessness causes us to stop being curious about basic contradictions like “Why do I work at Microsoft if I think war profiteering is despicable?” Our distraction is useful to the regime.

<br>

The handler (or the subject) of force is our state. The recipient (or the object) of force is overseas. We, the people are painfully trapped between, somehow guilty twofold: 

1) We condone the actions of our state, and sit idly, even feeding coal to its maw as it enacts the uncontrolled prerogative violence of Ernst Frankel’s dual state theory of dictatorship.

2) We enjoy the normative state resulting of #1.

<br>

We appear to have lost both ways. The pain we condone does not fully belong to us -- it is prescribed to us collectively by the empire, which exists on our tacit approval. And neither does the pleasure of the normative life even belong to us – it is reaped from the back of uncontrolled, arbitrary violence.

<br>

I also have to look away. It’s unbearable. After all, distraction allows the time to pass with less pain, and so at times, opting for distraction can be a temporarily humane choice. The pain comes not from the contradiction itself (which is a truth, not a sensation), but from the inability of the mind to reconcile the contradiction. In other words, the painful act is witnessing. The painful domain is our selves. 

<br>

But the time lost looking away neither dissolves the contradiction, nor can that time ever be recovered. After my flight from New York to Seattle, I couldn’t remember which movies I’d seen on the plane. I know vaguely that I had enjoyed them, but I couldn’t recall anything specific. The airplane movie which fled my mind like a dream as soon as it ended, did not stir me. It made me laugh at times, though about what, I can't remember. It had served its function, merely providing a place to rest my eyes, so that I could sit still for a while, slack-jawed, analgesic, despite my obvious discomfort. When the movies ended, I, the occluded witness could not remember them because they hardly even happened to me.

<br>

Pain relief is only a sensate benefit if the pain itself was felt first. Real absence precludes real presence. And deliberately looking away from the painful contradictions, while dissociative of pain, is also dissociative of all other aspects of the conscious life. It's hard, but it is important to look it in the eye. Not only to properly justify the ibuprofen (which is necessary at times), but to actually exist as a complex life form. Are you an amoeba? Are you simply a victim and beneficiary of ambient environmental nutrition? Does your pleasure belong to you? Does your pain? The looking, though painful, is essentially liberatory: it allows one to claim the sensations of witnessing for herself. I am not merely a member of a nation, nor a member of a market, nor a member of a life. Even if I feel helpless, I am the domain of perception. I am not the maker of reality, but I am the witness. I decide which pain I feel. It is not prescribed to me. And if or when the world delivers justice against the atrocities of this administration, that triumph will belong to me too. 

<br>

Why is war profiteering despicable? As a thought exercise, seek a reason beyond the sheer pathos-level disgust of benefitting from another’s death, suffering and harm. We know this pathos can become impotent in very complex systems like the modern private tech sector. Feelings-based empathy becomes an inconsistent proof for morality in such systems when individual actors are shielded from the brutality they create. 

<br>

Purist academic liberal and libertarian proponents of capitalism usually argue in a humanist way that private property and self-driven free markets create the greatest sum of overall human value by facilitating progressive trade and innovation. Perhaps war profiteering is despicable because it exposes this human value. We who are capitalists (by choice or by force) have all tacitly agreed that human suffering is valuable to us. It appears that it is worth our time. It appears that it is worth our time every day.
